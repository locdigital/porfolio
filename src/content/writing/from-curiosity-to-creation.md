Most of the things I know how to do today did not start with a formal plan. They started with a small question: *how does this actually work, and what happens if I try making one myself?*

When you begin from curiosity instead of credentials, there is rarely a clear roadmap. You look closely at something that catches your attention, take a rough first attempt, notice what broke, and try again with a slightly better guess. Over time, those small experiments start to connect.

## Learning before knowing the name for it

My first real lessons in marketing happened in ninth grade, long before I knew the word *marketing*. I spent time exploring how social media worked—why some posts travelled while others disappeared, what made people stay on a page, and why certain communities felt alive.

By the end of high school, I was building, buying, and selling Facebook pages. Back then, I wasn’t thinking about frameworks or funnels. I was simply figuring things out and finding practical ways to turn attention into something useful.

Studying Graphic Design at Arena and Digital Marketing at FPT University later gave names and structure to instincts I had already tested in the wild. Design taught me how layout and hierarchy shape the first three seconds of attention. Marketing taught me how to connect that attention to a measurable outcome.

## Building communities and chasing what happens after the click

As the projects grew larger, the questions changed. Co-founding communities like *Đại Học Đừng Học Đại* and *Đi Làm Đừng Đi Lầm* taught me that growth rarely comes from shouting louder; it comes from understanding what people are genuinely trying to decide at that moment in their lives.

Later, working across entertainment, education, coworking, luxury retail, and e-commerce—from *WorkFlow Space* to *PlayAh!*—made one lesson clear: good content gives performance marketing something worth scaling, and what happens after someone clicks matters just as much as the ad itself.

Every campaign, landing page, and tracking dashboard became another iteration of the same habit:

- **Notice the friction:** find where interest stops turning into progress.
- **Test one real change:** adjust the creative, the offer, or the page flow instead of guessing blindly.
- **Keep what works:** carry the lesson into the next round.

## Keeping the beginner’s habit alive

The same thread runs through the things I do outside client work. Long before I owned a camera, I used to make a rectangle with my fingers to frame whatever caught my eye. Eventually that habit pushed me to save up for my first camera, and today photography and highland trekking still give me a reason to slow down and pay attention.

Lately, that curiosity has pulled me into AI tools and building websites through vibe coding. Even when the tools change, the process feels familiar: start small, stay close to the details, figure out why something feels off, and keep refining until it works cleanly.

> You rarely have everything figured out at the beginning. Most meaningful work is just curiosity, stayed with long enough to become something real.

Looking back, none of those steps felt dramatic on the day they happened. Taken together, they turned into a way of working—one built around staying curious enough to start, and patient enough to make things work one step at a time.
