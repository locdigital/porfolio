Most people treat investing like a timing game. They wait for a pullback, read five conflicting macro threads, hesitate when the market looks "too high," and then freeze when it actually drops because the headlines suddenly feel scary.

In both cases, they end up doing the same thing: sitting on the sidelines while time—the one variable that actually does the heavy lifting—quietly slips away.

*Just keep buying* is the antidote to that paralysis. It is not a clever trick. It is a decision to replace prediction with a schedule.

## Timing feels smart, consistency builds wealth

Trying to call the bottom flatters the ego. If you get it right once, you feel like a genius. The problem is that waiting for the perfect entry usually costs more than buying at an imperfect price.

Markets spend most of their time moving upward over the long run, interrupted by stretches of volatility that nobody can time reliably. When you buy on a fixed cadence—every paycheck, every month, regardless of the mood on your feed—three useful things happen:

- **You remove the hardest decision:** you never have to wake up and ask whether today is a "good" day to invest. The calendar already answered.
- **You turn volatility into a feature:** when prices dip, the same contribution buys more shares; when prices rise, everything you already own grows.
- **You protect your attention:** instead of refreshing charts, you can put your energy into increasing your income, which matters far more in the early years than squeezing out an extra percent of return.

## Income is the engine, buying is the flywheel

In the beginning, your savings rate and earning power move the needle much faster than your portfolio ever will. No asset allocation fixes a flat income.

That is why the philosophy works best as a two-part system:

1. **Grow what you earn:** build rare skills, ship real work, and widen the gap between what you make and what you spend.
2. **Automate where the surplus goes:** move cash into productive, diversified assets on repeat before lifestyle creep or second-guessing can claim it.

Done for a few months, it looks boring. Done for a decade, it looks unfair.

## Staying in the game

The hardest part of *just keep buying* isn't understanding the math—it's surviving the stretches where everything is red and the smartest-sounding voices online insist that this time is different.

You don't need to predict the next crash or the next rally. You only need a plan simple enough to follow when you are busy, tired, or uncertain—and the patience to let compounding finish what consistency started.

> The best time to buy is rarely when it feels comfortable. It's whenever your system says it's time—again and again, for years.
