There is a particular kind of exhaustion that doesn't come from doing too much hard work—it comes from spending eight hours a day on tasks you secretly suspect wouldn't matter if they vanished tomorrow.

David Graeber's *Bullshit Jobs* put a sharp name to that quiet frustration. His argument wasn't that people are lazy; it was that modern organizations have become remarkably good at inventing layers of performative work—reports written to justify other reports, meetings scheduled to align on the next meeting, and vanity metrics polished so everyone in the room can pretend progress happened.

Once you notice how much energy gets burned on theater, it becomes impossible to unsee.

## How performative work creeps in

Almost no company sets out to create pointless work. It usually grows slowly as teams scale and distance themselves from the customer:

- **Measuring activity instead of outcomes:** when nobody is sure what actually drives revenue or retention, hours logged, slides produced, and quick Slack replies become the default scorecard.
- **Protecting status over truth:** decks get longer and jargon gets thicker not because the problem is complex, but because sounding busy feels safer than admitting a campaign or feature isn't working.
- **Fixing symptoms instead of causes:** entire roles end up dedicated to patching recurring breakdowns that could have been eliminated once at the root.

The cost isn't just wasted budget. Doing work you don't believe in quietly erodes your standards, your curiosity, and your pride in craft.

## Staying close to real outcomes

The best antidote to performative work isn't cynicism—it is proximity to reality. Whether you are running ads, designing a product, or building a small site from scratch, you can tell almost immediately when work is real because reality pushes back.

Three habits keep work honest:

1. **Follow the output all the way to the user:** don't stop at shipping a deck or launching an ad set. Watch what real people click, buy, ignore, or complain about.
2. **Prefer small, verifiable wins over grand theatre:** one landing page fix that lifts conversion rate beats a forty-slide quarterly strategy presentation every time.
3. **Kill unnecessary rituals early:** if a recurring meeting, tracker, or approval step hasn't changed a single decision in a month, stop feeding it.

## Choosing work that leaves a mark

Not every job can be romantic, and even meaningful work has boring admin attached to it. The difference is whether the boring parts exist to support something real—or whether the bureaucracy *is* the whole product.

Time and attention are too finite to spend acting out a role you know is hollow. Whenever you have the leverage to choose, move closer to the work where your effort visibly changes an outcome for someone on the other side of the screen.

> Real work doesn't need performance to prove it matters. You can feel it in the result—and in the quiet satisfaction of knowing that what you built actually worked.
