When you are inside a dark stretch, the hardest part isn't just the weight of the day itself—it is how convincing that weight becomes. A bad season has a way of editing your memory and your imagination at the same time, until it feels like things have always been this heavy and will stay that way forever.

Matt Haig’s *Reasons to Stay Alive* resonated with so many people because it refuses to romanticize that feeling or lecture its way past it. Instead, it names a simple truth that is easy to forget when your mind is tired: weather is not climate, and how a chapter feels from the middle is rarely how the whole story ends.

You don't get through those stretches by forcing yourself to feel grateful on command. You get through them by widening the frame, one small detail at a time.

## Heavy seasons shrink the frame

Anyone who has spent time behind a camera knows how much a lens changes reality. Zoom in tight enough on a patch of storm cloud and the entire sky looks black, even when light is already breaking over the next ridge.

Exhaustion, burnout, grief, and quiet self-doubt do the exact same thing to attention:

- **They flatten time:** a rough week starts masquerading as your permanent future, making temporary problems feel irreversible.
- **They mute curiosity:** the things you normally care about—craft, places, conversations, plans—temporarily lose their color, which tricks you into thinking the meaning is gone for good.
- **They isolate the struggle:** everyone else's life looks effortless from the outside, while your own effort feels invisible.

Recognizing that distortion doesn't make a hard season vanish overnight, but it gives you just enough distance not to trust every dark conclusion your mind offers at 2 a.m.

## Small anchors carry more weight than grand speeches

When people talk about reasons to keep going, they often reach for huge, cinematic milestones—career breakthroughs, big accolades, dramatic turning points. In practice, those are almost never what pull you through an ordinary Tuesday.

The real anchors are much smaller and much closer to the ground:

1. **Sensory, unhurried moments:** the first cup of iced coffee on a quiet street before the city wakes up, rain cooling hot pavement in Saigon, or cold mountain air hitting your face before sunrise on a trail.
2. **Things left unfinished in the best way:** books still sitting on your desk, songs you haven't heard for the first time yet, roads you haven't ridden, and photographs you haven't framed.
3. **Showing up without needing a breakthrough:** letting a day count simply because you took a walk, answered a friend, ate a proper meal, and gave tomorrow another chance.

None of those moments look impressive from a distance. Up close, they are what remind you that being alive isn't a performance you have to win—it is something you are allowed to experience at human speed.

## Staying for the versions of you that haven't arrived yet

Looking back at my own resets—times when plans stalled, directions changed, or energy ran dry—almost every meaningful chapter in my life happened *after* a stretch where I couldn't yet see what came next. Places I hadn't visited yet, friendships that hadn't formed yet, work I didn't even have the skills to imagine yet—all of them were waiting on the other side of simply staying in the room.

Curiosity is often gentler and more durable than forced optimism. You don't have to believe that everything will magically turn easy tomorrow. You only have to stay curious enough to find out what the view looks like once the fog lifts.

> You don't have to see the whole horizon today. Staying for the next quiet morning, the next honest conversation, and the future versions of yourself you haven't met yet is more than enough.
